## Target customer

**Two-sided**, genuinely so on both the feature set and the fee structure (see Pricing model): court reporters, videographers, and stenographers on the supply side; attorneys, paralegals, and firm scheduling staff on the demand side. Both sides have real accounts, not just a search box for one side and a claim flow for the other.

## Pricing model

- **Reporters:** free to list – "No sign-up fee. No subscription fees." Reporters keep the difference between what they charge and eCourt's cut:
  - **5% retained by eCourt** if the reporter handles their own transcript production
  - **An additional 15% (20% total)** if eCourt Reporters provides production services instead
- **Attorneys / firms:** searching, browsing, seeing reporters' **upfront rates**, and creating an account are **FREE** — "no sign-up fee, no subscription fees" for attorneys/paralegals (confirmed 2026-08-04, attorney-benefits page). The **10% of invoice total** applies **only when a firm actually books + pays** through the platform. **→ On ACCESS, eCourt is *more open* than DS on two dimensions:** free discovery (DS gates search for *new* reporters behind the $49/mo sub) and open, upfront rate visibility (DS's rates are opt-in per reporter + Pro-firm-gated). **DS's counter:** $0 at the transaction (vs. their 10%) + the complete pre-loaded directory they structurally lack.
- **Billing mechanics:** a separate "Make a Payment" flow exists for invoices; not confirmed whether this runs through a named processor (e.g., Stripe) – not disclosed on the pages fetched this pass.

**This is a percentage-of-transaction model on both sides at once (10% from the buyer, 5-20% from the seller) – a different structure from DS's current model. DS's firms pay a flat $49/user/month (or $539/year) subscription that gates searching for *new* reporters, with no per-transaction fee at all; browsing, messaging, paying, and saving reporters a firm is already connected to is free. DS's reporters claim, list, and message for free, and only pay a small fee when they actually collect a payment through the platform (5% on the free tier, or 2% plus a flat $29/month on Pro). eCourt is still the closest fee-model peer to DS in this competitive set**, in the specific sense that it's the only other product that charges for the transaction itself rather than just the listing – the mechanics differ meaningfully (working DS numbers; confirm before quoting externally). Every other directory/marketplace profiled so far (StenoScout, CourtReporters.com) monetizes purely through reporter listing subscriptions and never touches the transaction. eCourt Reporters is the only one that takes a cut of the actual job – which also means it's the only one of DS's directory/marketplace peers with a plausible answer to "how do you make real money once volume shows up," the same question DS itself is built around.

**Confidence note:** these percentages are quoted directly from eCourt Reporters' own site copy (fetched 2026-08-04), not independently verified against a live invoice or a reporter's actual account. Treat as "what they publicly claim," not confirmed fact.

## CSR-supply model

**Self-claimed profiles, not seeded from public license data.** Reporters "create a profile" listing "specialty services, credentials, availability, and rates" and can update it anytime – a standard claim-your-listing flow, not a directory pre-populated from a state licensing board the way DS's CA directory is built from CRB data. **Reporters independently set and display their own rates** on their profile, which attorneys can search by (the site copy specifically lists "rates" as a searchable filter alongside name, location, and qualifications) – this is a real point of overlap with DS's rate-transparency positioning, and arguably ahead of DS on one dimension: rates are visible during search/browse, not just at the match-preview stage after a request is made.

**Reporters keep their own client relationship on the surface** – the platform frames bookings as attorneys booking "you directly" – but eCourt Reporters sits in the middle of the money (5-20% retained) in a way DS's core listing doesn't. This is a real structural difference worth being precise about: the *introduction* is direct on both platforms, and DS does route reporter get-paid tooling (invoicing, payment) through the platform too – but DS's cut of that payment is a smaller per-payment fee (5% free tier, or 2% plus a flat $29/month on Pro) rather than eCourt's 5-20%, and DS never takes a cut of the firm's side of the transaction at all.

## Strengths vs. DS

- **The only directory/marketplace peer with a real, working transaction-fee model on both sides** – proof that charging for the transaction itself (not just the listing) is workable in this exact category, which is directly relevant validation for the transaction-fee half of DS's own model: the reporter-side per-payment fee DS charges when a reporter collects money through the platform. DS deliberately doesn't charge firms a per-transaction cut the way eCourt does – DS's firm-side revenue is a flat $49/user/month subscription instead.
- **In-platform messaging confirmed** – matches DS's own core differentiator against StenoScout and CourtReporters.com (both lead-gen-only). eCourt Reporters is not thin on this dimension the way the other directories are.
- **Rates visible at search/browse time**, not just at a later match-preview step – arguably a small UX edge over DS's current flow (rate visibility at match-preview, post-submit).
- **Free to list for reporters – same as DS, so not actually a point of difference.** Both platforms let a reporter claim a profile at no cost. The real difference is what happens after a reporter gets paid: eCourt takes 5-20% of every job indefinitely, while DS takes a smaller per-payment fee only when a reporter actually collects money through the platform (5% on the free tier, or 2% plus a flat $29/month on Pro). Worth having this comparison ready for reporter-recruiting conversations, since "free to list" alone won't differentiate DS from eCourt.

## Weaknesses vs. DS – where DS specifically wins

- **10% fee lands on the firm on top of the reporter's own rate, on every single job, forever** – a real, compounding transaction cost every time a firm books through eCourt. DS doesn't charge firms a transaction fee at all: browsing, messaging, paying, and saving reporters a firm is already connected to is free, and the only firm-side cost is a flat $49/user/month (or $539/year) subscription that gates searching for *new* reporters – a predictable subscription with zero per-job tax, meaningfully cheaper than eCourt's uncapped 10%-of-every-invoice model for any firm doing real volume.
- **Reporters give up 5-20% of every job, indefinitely** – DS's per-payment fee is lower and only ever charged on money a reporter actually collects: 5% on the free tier (no monthly cost at all) or 2% plus a flat $29/month on Pro, with nothing owed in a month a reporter doesn't get paid. This is a real, quantifiable pitch DS can make directly to reporters currently on or considering eCourt Reporters: DS's Pro tier alone (2% vs. eCourt's 5-20%) is cheaper per job for any reporter doing real volume, on top of never paying anything just to be listed.
- **No day-one directory completeness.** Like every other claim-based directory in this competitive set, eCourt Reporters only lists reporters who've signed up – it has no equivalent to DS's CA directory being pre-populated from public CRB license data on day one. A firm searching eCourt Reporters in a CA county DS already covers completely may simply not find a reporter who hasn't joined yet.
- **No stated state-specific depth or compliance framing** (no CA statutory-rate-era language, no CRB verification, nothing IL-specific found) – DS's state-by-state depth (CA now, Illinois next) is a real differentiator against a thin-nationwide competitor.
- **Thinner public marketing footprint** – no per-city SEO content strategy found (unlike StenoScout), no funding or press coverage found, suggesting smaller scale or lower growth-marketing investment. Less of a discovery threat today, though this could change.
