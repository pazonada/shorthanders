## Target customer

**Two-sided**, genuinely so on both the feature set and the fee structure (see Pricing model): court reporters, videographers, and stenographers on the supply side; attorneys, paralegals, and firm scheduling staff on the demand side. Both sides have real accounts, not just a search box for one side and a claim flow for the other.

---

## Pricing model

- **Reporters:** free to list – "No sign-up fee. No subscription fees." Reporters keep the difference between what they charge and eCourt's cut:
  - **5% retained by eCourt** if the reporter handles their own transcript production
  - **An additional 15% (20% total)** if eCourt Reporters provides production services instead
- **Attorneys / firms:** **10% of invoice total** when a firm books through the platform
- **Billing mechanics:** a separate "Make a Payment" flow exists for invoices; not confirmed whether this runs through a named processor (e.g., Stripe) – not disclosed on the pages fetched this pass.

**This is a percentage-of-transaction model on both sides at once (10% from the buyer, 5-20% from the seller) – meaningfully different from DS's attorney-only 12% fee, and the closest fee-model peer to DS found in this competitive set.** Every other directory/marketplace profiled so far (StenoScout, CourtReporters.com) monetizes purely through reporter listing subscriptions and never touches the transaction. eCourt Reporters is the only one that takes a cut of the actual job – which also means it's the only one of DS's directory/marketplace peers with a plausible answer to "how do you make real money once volume shows up," the same question DS itself is built around.

**Confidence note:** these percentages are quoted directly from eCourt Reporters' own site copy (fetched 2026-08-04), not independently verified against a live invoice or a reporter's actual account. Treat as "what they publicly claim," not confirmed fact.

## CSR-supply model

**Self-claimed profiles, not seeded from public license data.** Reporters "create a profile" listing "specialty services, credentials, availability, and rates" and can update it anytime – a standard claim-your-listing flow, not a directory pre-populated from a state licensing board the way DS's CA directory is built from CRB data. **Reporters independently set and display their own rates** on their profile, which attorneys can search by (the site copy specifically lists "rates" as a searchable filter alongside name, location, and qualifications) – this is a real point of overlap with DS's rate-transparency positioning, and arguably ahead of DS on one dimension: rates are visible during search/browse, not just at the match-preview stage after a request is made.

**Reporters keep their own client relationship on the surface** – the platform frames bookings as attorneys booking "you directly" – but eCourt Reporters sits in the middle of the money (5-20% retained, invoice/payment flow through the platform) in a way DS's model doesn't for its core listing. This is a real structural difference worth being precise about: the *introduction* is direct (like DS), but the *transaction* is intermediated (unlike DS's messaging-plus-external-invoicing model, though DS does route reporter get-paid tooling through the platform too – the distinction is DS doesn't take a cut of what the reporter charges the firm beyond its own attorney-side fee).

## Strengths vs. DS

- **The only directory/marketplace peer with a real, working transaction-fee model on both sides** – proof a two-sided percentage fee is a workable structure in this exact category, which is directly relevant validation for DS's own attorney-side 12% fee.
- **In-platform messaging confirmed** – matches DS's own core differentiator against StenoScout and CourtReporters.com (both lead-gen-only). eCourt Reporters is not thin on this dimension the way the other directories are.
- **Rates visible at search/browse time**, not just at a later match-preview step – arguably a small UX edge over DS's current flow (rate visibility at match-preview, post-submit).
- **Free to list for reporters** – no listing fee at all, which is a lower-friction pitch to reporters than DS's $99/yr individual listing fee. Worth knowing this exists as a reporter-recruiting counter-argument, even though eCourt's model instead takes a percentage of every job (a different tradeoff, not necessarily cheaper for an active reporter).

---

## Weaknesses vs. DS – where DS specifically wins

- **10% fee lands on the firm on top of the reporter's own rate, with no visible free-to-browse-and-not-pay option beyond search** – firms pay a real transaction cost to use the platform at all, whereas DS's directory, profile browsing, and search are entirely free for firms with the 12% fee only applying at the point of an actual booking through the platform (same category of fee, but DS's is a single fee vs. eCourt's compounding 10%-plus-reporter-side-cut structure that likely gets passed through in reporter pricing anyway).
- **Reporters give up 5-20% of every job** – meaningfully more expensive for an active reporter than DS's flat $99/yr listing fee once volume is more than a few jobs a year. This is a real, quantifiable pitch DS can make directly to reporters currently on or considering eCourt Reporters: predictable flat cost vs. an uncapped percentage of every job.
- **No day-one directory completeness.** Like every other claim-based directory in this competitive set, eCourt Reporters only lists reporters who've signed up – it has no equivalent to DS's CA directory being pre-populated from public CRB license data on day one. A firm searching eCourt Reporters in a CA county DS already covers completely may simply not find a reporter who hasn't joined yet.
- **No stated state-specific depth or compliance framing** (no CA statutory-rate-era language, no CRB verification, nothing IL-specific found) – DS's state-by-state depth (CA now, Illinois next) is a real differentiator against a thin-nationwide competitor.
- **Thinner public marketing footprint** – no per-city SEO content strategy found (unlike StenoScout), no funding or press coverage found, suggesting smaller scale or lower growth-marketing investment. Less of a discovery threat today, though this could change.
